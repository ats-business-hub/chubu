Packing for Central Japan in winter means a warm coat, non-skid boots, and a bag you can carry on icy streets. Takayama and Shirakawa-go sit in the mountains of Gifu Prefecture. Official tourism pages treat December through March as snow country: streets ice over, buses can run late in heavy snow, and footwear matters as much as a warm coat.

The checklist below is sixteen clothing and gear items. Four more — an eSIM or pocket Wi-Fi, and two optional tours — are bookings, so the page covers twenty items in all. I have not walk-tested these streets or these products for this article. I am not inventing a temperature chart or naming boot brands. Recheck official pages before you go.

Takayama and Shirakawa-go winter packing checklist

Sixteen clothing and gear items, grouped the way you will actually pack. Clothing notes here stay short. The longer quotations and the source-by-source shoe list live on what to wear in Shirakawa-go and Takayama in winter. Small-gear Amazon lines are placeholders only.

1. Warm coat or down jacket

Hida Takayama’s official climate page says a warm coat or jacket is essential in December, January, and February, and still handy in November and March. SHIRAKAWA-Going recommends very warm clothing such as down jackets, and says some days can reach −5°C — the guide’s wording for some days, not a daily average I calculated.

2. Waterproofed coat, rain shell, or poncho

Shirakawa Village’s first-visit page recommends waterproofed coats. The village FAQ asks visitors to use rainwear or a poncho rather than an umbrella when it is snowing.

Packable rain shell or poncho (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Official FAQ prefers rainwear over an umbrella in falling snow.

3. Non-skid waterproof boots

Takayama strongly recommends non-skid boots so you can walk safely on snow and ice in December–February. Shirakawa Village asks for long boots or footwear with a deep, grippy tread, snow-type boots, and no heels, because streets are often icy even when it is not snowing. No official page names a model.

Non-skid waterproof winter boots (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Official guidance only: grippy, waterproof, no heels.

4. Light-up evenings: add spikes + headlamp

The Shirakawa-go Tourism Association’s 2027 light-up outline recommends spikes and a flashlight when evening temperatures sit around −10°C to 0°C. Add a headlamp as well as spikes: they supplement proper boots rather than replace them, and they help on ordinary icy streets too. Light-up entry is reservation-only and is not a highway-bus ticket; see Shirakawa-go winter light-up 2027.

5. Warm gloves, plus a spare pair

Official Takayama and village pages include gloves. SHIRAKAWA-Going notes that gloves can get wet on snow-covered roads, so pack a second pair.

Insulated gloves, with a spare pair (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Pack a second pair; official pages say gloves can get wet.

6. Warm hat

Takayama’s official December–February clothing list includes a hat, alongside a coat and gloves. A beanie is enough for town; a light-up evening is the colder case.

Warm hat or beanie (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Listed on Takayama’s official seasonal clothing notes.

7. Extra warm socks

The same official visitor guide says socks can get wet on snow-covered roads. Pack at least one dry spare pair for a village day.

Spare warm socks (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Official guide: socks can get wet; bring extras.

8. Thermal base layers

Official pages do not name base layers. This is practical kit for the gap between daytime and night-time temperatures, and heat-tech style layers are easy to buy in Nagoya if you would rather pack light.

Thermal base layers (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Practical, not an official named item. Easy to buy in Nagoya too.

9. Waterproof trousers or lower-body rainwear

Useful for slush, bus-stop spray, and short walks through wet snow. The village warns visitors not to step onto uncleared fresh snow, which can hide holes, ponds, or irrigation ditches.

Waterproof trousers or rain pants (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Practical kit for slush and wet snow.

10. Two-flat-pin plug adapter

JNTO says the voltage throughout Japan is uniformly 100 volts AC — 60 Hz in western Japan, including Nagoya, and 50 Hz in eastern Japan. Dual-voltage chargers usually need only an adapter; UK, EU, and AU plugs need one, and many US two-flat-pin plugs already fit. Source: JNTO, Plugs & Electricity.

Japan two-flat-pin travel adapter (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Japan is 100V AC (JNTO). Dual-voltage USB chargers usually need only an adapter.

11. Power bank

Cold drains phones, and a pocket Wi-Fi router needs charging too. Nohi warns that delays of two hours or more can occur in snow or when expressways close, so a reserve charge matters more than on a short city day.

Portable power bank (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Helpful in the cold and on delayed winter buses.

12. IC card holder

A physical IC card (Manaca in Nagoya, or another nationwide-compatible card such as Suica) is handy for Meitetsu, many local buses, and some lockers. Buy or charge the card in Japan; this item is only a holder. A mobile IC card is an alternative if your phone supports it.

IC card holder (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Buy or charge the transit card in Japan; this is only a sleeve.

13. Packable daypack

Leave the main suitcase at the hotel, or in luggage storage at Takayama Station, and carry only what you need on the Shirakawa-go bus. See Takayama Station luggage storage.

Packable daypack (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. For the village bus day, after you store the main bag.

14. Hand warmers

Optional comfort on a −5°C village day, or on a colder light-up evening. Disposable kairo hand warmers are widely sold in Japanese convenience stores and pharmacies, so you can buy them after you arrive.

Hand warmers (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Optional; easy to buy in Japan too.

15. Sunglasses

Snow can glare on a bright day, including on the walk between the bus park and the houses. A simple pair is enough; no official page names a brand.

Sunglasses (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. For snow glare on a bright village day.

16. Lip balm or moisturiser

Takayama’s official climate page notes relatively low humidity and a wide gap between daytime and night-time temperatures, so lips and skin dry out. A small balm covers a village day.

Lip balm or moisturiser (Amazon.com — placeholder)Not a live link. Official notes mention low humidity.

What to buy in Japan instead of packing

  • Extra heat-tech style layers and socks in Nagoya, if the suitcase is tight.
  • Disposable hand warmers at convenience stores and pharmacies.
  • Onsen towels at inns and shops in Takayama or Gero. See Gero Onsen day trip if that is on the plan.
  • A conversion plug or transformer in a large electronics shop, if you forgot one — JNTO notes they are relatively easy to find in urban areas.

Shirakawa-go day-trip kit (what to carry)

If you already have the coat and boots, this is the smaller bag for a Shirakawa-go day from Takayama or Nagoya:

  • Daypack, spare socks and gloves, traction spikes if the forecast is icy.
  • Power bank, and the pocket Wi-Fi router if you rented one.
  • Headlamp if you may still be out near dusk, or if you have a reserved light-up evening.
  • Water, and any snacks you want — pack rubbish out of the village.
  • Offline maps, in case coverage drops in the valleys.

Frequently asked questions

What should I pack for Takayama and Shirakawa-go in winter?
Sixteen clothing and gear items cover the bag: a warm coat, non-skid waterproof boots, gloves, a hat, spare socks, and, for a light-up evening, spikes and a headlamp. Four further items are bookings rather than clothes: a Japan eSIM or pocket Wi-Fi before you fly, plus an optional day tour or city tour. That is twenty items in all. This is a researched checklist, not a product test.
What shoes should I wear in Shirakawa-go in winter?
Shirakawa Village’s official English pages advise against heels and recommend long boots, snow-type boots, or shoes with a deep, grippy tread, because streets are often icy even when it is not snowing. Hida Takayama’s official climate page strongly recommends non-skid boots in December–February. The village’s SHIRAKAWA-Going guide also recommends waterproof boots. No official page names a brand.
Should I book an eSIM or a pocket Wi-Fi for a Chubu winter trip?
An eSIM suits a traveller with an unlocked, eSIM-capable phone who wants to be online on arrival, with no device to carry or return. A pocket Wi-Fi router is easier to share in a group, or when someone’s phone cannot take an eSIM. Confirm pickup hours at Chubu Centrair — the airport post office is weekdays 9:00–17:00 only — and compare plan length and fair-use rules before you pay.
Do I need a voltage converter in Japan?
JNTO says the voltage throughout Japan is uniformly 100 volts AC. Western Japan, including Nagoya, uses 60 Hz; eastern Japan uses 50 Hz. Dual-voltage chargers, hair dryers, and shavers should work with a two-flat-pin adapter only. Other appliances may need a step-down transformer. UK, EU, and AU plugs need an adapter; many US two-flat-pin plugs already fit.
